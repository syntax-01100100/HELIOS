HELIOS · Health Evaluation & Life Intelligence for Orbital Safety

Intelligent Health Monitoring. Safer Space Exploration.

HELIOS is a helmet-integrated module that reads an astronaut’s health signals, analyses them on the device and warns through the visor, vibration and sound, even when Earth is minutes away. This is an interactive prototype simulation, not a medical device.

01 · SYSTEM OVERVIEW

What is HELIOS?

HELIOS is a compact helmet-integrated processing module designed to be installed in an astronaut’s helmet or a compatible spacesuit electronics compartment. It receives data from physiological sensors, a helmet camera and suit telemetry, processes it locally and presents the results on the visor.

The system is designed to identify unusual changes in physiological readings, provide contextual alerts and make essential health and emergency information accessible without separate handheld devices.

01

Physiological Monitoring

  • Heart rate (BPM)
  • Blood oxygen saturation (SpO₂)
  • Skin temperature
  • Body movement and activity
02

Offline Intelligence

By design, an onboard computing unit processes sensor readings without a network link. It compares them with the astronaut’s individual baseline and flags unusual changes. (This web demo needs an internet connection to load its camera-vision models.)

03

Augmented Reality Interface

The display shows heart rate, SpO₂, skin temperature, movement, spacesuit oxygen and pressure indicators (simulated), system status and contextual recommendations in the field of view.

04

Multimodal Alert System

Visual indicators, vibration and audible alerts communicate notifications through several sensory channels, giving alternative methods for different operating circumstances.

05

Emergency Assistance

In a simulated emergency, HELIOS shows instructions, emergency-kit information and alerts to nearby connected units. Satellite communication to mission control is a conceptual feature.

06

Astronaut Authentication

Access to health information and controls is restricted through a conceptual interface using facial recognition, fingerprint verification or voice authentication.

Why HELIOS matters

Astronauts work in restrictive suits on complex tasks, and separate monitoring equipment can interrupt operations. On deep-space missions, help is also delayed by minutes. HELIOS explores how helmet-embedded electronics and augmented reality could put health information in front of the astronaut immediately, support early awareness of unusual changes and improve communication in emergencies.

Potential future applications

  • Deep-sea divers
  • Mountain expedition teams
  • Search-and-rescue personnel
  • Emergency response workers
  • Remote health monitoring

Current status: conceptual prototype and software simulation. These applications are exploratory and have not been built or tested.

02 · WHY HELIOS

The problem, and what makes HELIOS different.

On a deep-space mission, help cannot arrive in real time. A radio message to Mars can take up to 22 minutes each way, and a Mars transit has no evacuation option. Spacewalk health data has traditionally been watched by flight surgeons on the ground. HELIOS asks a different question: what if the astronaut’s own helmet could notice a change and warn them within seconds?

COMMUNICATION DELAY

Up to 22 min

One-way radio delay to Mars in NASA’s reference mission. Real-time medical consultation with Earth is not possible.

MEDICAL DISTANCE

No evacuation

NASA’s Mars transit reference mission has no evacuation capability, so crews must rely on their own resources.

DESIGN RULE

At least 2 senses

Every HELIOS alert is designed to reach the astronaut through two or more channels: sight, touch or sound. This is a design goal and still needs testing with real users.

How HELIOS compares with existing approaches

APPROACHWhat it does today · The gap HELIOS explores
Ground-monitored spacewalk telemetryHeart rate and ECG during International Space Station spacewalks have been observed by flight surgeons on the ground. Gap: the data goes to Earth first. With a multi-minute delay at Mars, the astronaut needs a local warning.
Smart garments (Astroskin / Hexoskin Bio-Monitor)A smart shirt used on the ISS since 2019 that records vital signs, sleep and activity for research and sends data to Earth. Gap: built for research and ground analysis, not a visor display with immediate multi-sense alerts.
Next-generation suits (e.g. Axiom AxEMU)Onboard biometric monitoring and diagnostics; 2024 reports describe a handheld display rather than a helmet HUD. Gap: HELIOS explores a visor display and alert layer that could complement such suit systems. HELIOS is not NASA or Axiom hardware.
Helmet HUD concepts and student designsConcepts such as Hypergiant’s HyperVSR and NASA SUITS student teams show vitals and suit data on a helmet display. Gap: HELIOS focuses on what happens after a reading changes: baseline comparison, vibration and audio alerts, accessibility and crew-to-crew alerts.

What is new in HELIOS

A helmet display on its own is not new. HELIOS contributes a combination of five ideas in one prototype.

01

Astronaut first, ground second

Analysis is designed to happen on the helmet, offline, so the astronaut is warned even when the link to Earth is slow or lost.

02

Personal baseline

Readings are designed to be compared with that astronaut’s own normal, not only one fixed limit for everyone.

03

Inclusive alerts

Visual, vibration and audio channels let deaf, hard-of-hearing and low-vision crew receive the same warning. Testing with these users is planned.

04

Honest data labels

Every value is labelled simulated, sensor or camera-derived. In hardware mode HELIOS shows nothing unless a real sensor sends data.

05

Crew-to-crew awareness

Buddy Link lets crew members share caution and critical alerts, so help does not depend only on Earth.

Targets and expected impact (estimates)

BLOOD OXYGEN TARGET

About 97%

Readings within ±3 percentage points of a hospital reference, which is the medical standard for pulse oximeters.

HEART RATE TARGET

About 96%

Within ±3 beats per minute at a resting rate of about 70.

SKIN TEMPERATURE TARGET

About 99%

Within ±0.3 °C of 36.6 °C. This is skin temperature, not core body temperature.

ALERT TARGET

90%+ caught

Goal: catch at least 9 in 10 real unusual events, with few false alarms. Unknown until tested.

WARNING SPEED

99.9% faster

If an alert appears in about 2 seconds, it beats a 44-minute Mars round trip to Earth by about 99.9%. Design target.

MARKET (OPTIMISTIC)

$10–100M a year

About 1–3% of the smart-helmet market (estimated at $1.3–3.9 billion by 2030), reached years after launch.

These are design targets and estimates, not measured results. HELIOS has not been tested on real people. Market sizes come from public analyst reports and vary widely.

    07 · TECHNICAL SPECIFICATIONS

    Conceptual architecture

    SENSINGOptical pulse and SpO₂ using red and infrared LEDs with a photodiode; ECG electrodes with an analog front-end chip; digital skin-temperature sensor; motion chip; breathing rate estimated from ECG or pulse. SpO₂ is about ±2–3% at best and degraded by motion. Skin temperature is not core body temperature.
    SUIT AIRCO₂, O₂ and pressure sensors. Rising CO₂ is a real suit hazard.
    RADIATIONSeparate silicon-detector dosimeter reporting dose rate in µGy/h, the same unit as NASA RadLab. Neutrons need their own detector. HELIOS measures exposure, not biological damage.
    PROCESSINGLow-power ARM Cortex-M microcontroller for vitals, filters and alerts; a separate application-class chip with a small AI accelerator for camera vision. The ESP32 is a ground prototype board only, not a flight part.
    SOFTWARE PIPELINEClean the signal and detect each heartbeat → compare with the astronaut’s own baseline → combine signals (fast pulse with falling SpO₂ and rising CO₂ points to a suit problem; fast pulse during exercise does not) → tiered alerts: advisory, caution, warning → visor, audio, vibration.
    SPACE RELIABILITYRadiation can flip bits in ordinary chips. Flight hardware uses error-correcting memory, a watchdog reset, redundant processors and shielding.
    AUTONOMYMars one-way delay is about 3–22 minutes, so the helmet decides locally and sends summaries when the link allows.
    DISPLAYHelmet visor display layer driven by the module
    ALERTSVisual, vibration and audio channels
    POWERHelmet power supply with on-module power management (simulated)
    COMMUNICATIONShort-range link to nearby units and satellite relay (conceptual)
    INSTALLATIONCompact enclosure with four mounting points in a helmet electronics compartment, wired to the visor, camera, audio, haptics, suit telemetry and skin-contact sensors
    ACCESSFace, fingerprint or voice authentication (conceptual)
    STATUSSoftware simulation only. Not a medical device. Readings are simulated.

    How it could really be built

    1. Bench prototype.
    2. Accuracy testing against medical-grade ECG and pulse oximeters on volunteers.
    3. Analog missions and ground tests.
    4. Safety certification against NASA human-system standards.
    5. Flight demo on the ISS.
    Honest claims

    Each part exists today. Our contribution is the integration, per-astronaut baselines, and decisions made on the helmet itself. HELIOS detects changes and exposure, then alerts. It does not detect everything or diagnose illness. NASA data shows the hazards are real and gives realistic conditions to demonstrate against; our alert limits are demo values and a design concept to be validated with NASA experts.

    How a heartbeat becomes a warning.

    A sensor at a skin-contact point reads the astronaut’s pulse, the HELIOS module decides what it means, and the helmet warns them in more than one way.

    Reality check

    These are rough typical values, not measurements. The wireless radio and the vibration motor use the most, so real modules sleep the radio and buzz only briefly. Everything runs at once, so real runtime is shorter than any single bar.
